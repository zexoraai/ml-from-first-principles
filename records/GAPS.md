# GAPS — known limitations, open risks, and things we are not claiming

Honest register of what is missing. This file is the antidote to portfolio inflation: anything a
visitor might reasonably assume, but which is not true, belongs here and on the relevant page.

---

## G-001 — No CUDA device on the development machine
**Severity:** high · **Affects:** P2 (scale), P5 (Triton), P6 (low-bit compute), P7/P8 (training time)

`nvidia-smi` is absent; the GPU is an integrated AMD Radeon (Vega) under Windows. Consequences:
- Triton kernels cannot be compiled or benchmarked locally (see DECISIONS D-004).
- All training is CPU training, so every experiment is scale-limited by wall clock, not by ideas.
- Any claim about GPU kernel performance must come from an external runtime and be labelled with
  that runtime's hardware.

**Exact remaining requirement to close this gap:** access to one NVIDIA GPU with CUDA ≥ 12 and
Triton support (a free Colab T4 session suffices for P5 correctness + timing; a rented A10/A100
hour would be needed for the P2 124 M target).

**Severity:** medium · **Affects:** any run > 10 min

## G-005 — Attention visualisations are not causal explanations
**Severity:** conceptual, must be stated on P1's page

Attention weights show *where a weighted average drew from*, at one layer, in one head, for one
input. They do not establish that the information was used, that the head is doing the job the
picture suggests, or that changing the input would change the output as implied. Residual streams
mix information across layers; a head with high weight on a token can still contribute nothing to
the logit that matters. Establishing use requires intervention (ablation, patching), not
inspection. P1's page will say this next to the visualiser, not in a footnote.

## G-009 — Project 1's attention maps are displayed but not validated as explanations
**Severity:** conceptual, stated on the page · **Affects:** P1's demo, and every later project with a
head viewer

The demo renders real attention weights from a real forward pass. That is a faithful record of a
computation. It is **not** evidence that any head does the job its picture suggests, and the page says
so directly next to the viewer rather than in a footnote.

What would be needed to upgrade the hypothesis "head 2 of layer 1 locates the year" into a result:
ablate the head and measure the change in the target logit, or patch its activations from a different
input and check whether the prediction follows. Neither has been run. No number in P1's Results
section depends on any interpretation of the maps.

This is deliberately unresolved rather than quietly fixed, because the honest version of an
interpretability demo is one that shows the computation and refuses the causal claim.

## G-010 — Project 1's trained model is a single seed on a synthetic task
**Severity:** medium · **Affects:** every P1 metric

One run, one seed (1706), no variance estimate. The task is generated by our own code with exactly
one correct answer per input, so high exact-match is evidence the mechanisms work — not evidence of
language ability.

**Exact remaining requirement:** two more seeds (~30 min each on this hardware) would give a range
rather than a point. Worth doing before any claim rests on a small difference; nothing currently
does. The pre-norm/post-norm comparison in the mastery pack is deliberately framed as a prediction
exercise rather than a reported result for exactly this reason.

## G-006 — Browser demo parity risk
**Severity:** medium · **Affects:** every demo (D-003)

A hand-written JS forward pass or an ONNX export can silently diverge from the trained PyTorch
model. Unmitigated, this turns "genuine model output" into a fabrication. **Mitigation is
mandatory, not optional:** each demo ships a parity fixture (fixed inputs → PyTorch outputs) and
the page publishes the measured max absolute deviation and the tolerance.

---

## G-008 — The development machine is not an isolated benchmark host
**Severity:** high for any timing claim · **Affects:** every measured number in this portfolio

At the time of the first benchmarks, `docker ps` showed **37 unrelated containers running**, all up
seven hours: OpenSearch 2.19, ClamAV 1.4, rspamd, ten-ish Postgres/pgvector instances, several
Redis, MinIO, MongoDB, NATS, Temporal (server + UI), LiteLLM, Mailpit, and multiple application
services. They belong to the user's other projects and are **not ours to stop**.

Measured impact — the same workload, minutes apart:

| Measurement | Quieter moment | Contended moment | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2048² fp32 matmul, 6 threads | 95.8 ms (179.4 GFLOP/s) | 266.6 ms (64.4 GFLOP/s) | 2.8x |
| 256² fp32 matmul, 4 threads | 1.36 ms | 19.17 ms | 14x |
| 0.53 M-param training step, 2 threads | 100.5 ms | 197.7 ms | 2.0x |

Consequences, applied consistently from here on:
1. **Every timing is a lower bound** on this hardware's capability, and is labelled as such.
2. Every benchmark record carries a load snapshot (`labs/common/sysload.py`) so an outlier is
   annotated rather than mysterious.
3. Extrapolated training durations are reported as a **range** spanning quiet and contended
   throughput, never as a single number.
4. Thread-count conclusions are only reported where they hold across *both* contention regimes.
   The one that does: the tiny configuration is fastest at **2 threads**, and 6 threads is
   actively harmful for it. That reproduced in every run.

**Exact remaining requirement to close this gap:** one benchmarking window with the unrelated
container stack stopped (`docker stop $(docker ps -q)` — the user's call, not ours, since those
services hold state and have been up for hours). Until then, treat all absolute timings as
"achievable while the dev stack is running".

## G-011 — Project 4's preferences are constructed, not human-annotated

**Severity:** high, and it bounds what P4 may claim · **Affects:** every P4 number

There is no human preference data in this portfolio. Collecting it properly needs annotators, an
annotation interface, inter-annotator agreement measurement, and a budget — none of which exist here.

What was done instead: preferences are generated by a **documented, deterministic rule** over the
licensed design corpus. `chosen` is the real passage sitting under a topic heading; `rejected` is one of
four degradations of it (`off_topic`, `truncated`, `repetitive`, `generic`). The rule lives in
`labs/p4_dpo/data.py`, is emitted into every run's `result.json` manifest, and must appear on the
project page.

**Why this is still a valid experiment.** The preference signal is real and verifiable — on-topic,
complete, non-repetitive prose genuinely is preferable to its degradations — and the labels are correct
by construction rather than by opinion. So it is a sound test of whether *the DPO mechanism moves a
policy toward a stated preference*, which is the tier-E claim actually being made.

**What it is not, and what must never be implied.** It measures nothing about human values,
helpfulness, harmlessness, or truthfulness. A model that improves here has learned "prefer on-topic
complete prose over four specific corruptions" and nothing more. Any sentence on the P4 page that
sounds like alignment-with-human-intent is a lie, and reviewers should treat one as such.

**A second-order limitation:** the evaluation detectors are the *same rules* that constructed the
degradations, applied in reverse. That alignment is deliberate — it means a change in the metric is a
change in exactly the thing that was optimised — but it also means the detectors are blind to any
failure mode the degradations did not model.

**What would close it:** a genuine annotation effort, or adopting a public human-preference dataset
(Anthropic HH-RLHF, OpenAssistant) and reporting on it. The latter is cheap and is the obvious upgrade
once a GPU is available; it is blocked now only because a 2.9 M-parameter model on 6.2 M characters
cannot produce responses worth ranking.

## G-012 — Project 3's LoRA-vs-full comparison uses different learning rates per arm

**Severity:** medium, and it is the largest caveat on P3's quality numbers · **Affects:** P3

LoRA and the full fine-tune are trained at **different learning rates** (`--lora-lr` default 1e-3,
`--full-lr` default 1e-4), both recorded in `result.json`.

This is not sloppiness, it is the lesser of two evils. The adapter is initialised so that `BA = 0` and
its contribution is scaled by `α/r`, so at a learning rate suited to full fine-tuning it barely moves.
Standard LoRA practice is a markedly higher LR for exactly this reason. Forcing both arms onto one LR
would handicap whichever arm that value suited less, and the quality comparison would measure the LR
choice rather than the method.

**The residual risk:** neither LR was extensively swept. A quality gap of the size P3 reports could
therefore be an LR artefact rather than a property of LoRA. The parameter-count, optimizer-state,
gradient-byte, and shipped-size numbers are **not** affected — those are exact and are P3's real
result. The *quality* comparison is the soft part.

**What would close it:** a small LR sweep per arm, reporting each arm's best. Roughly 3 × 2 extra runs;
deferred because it is a cloud-phase task (D-009) and does not change the memory findings.

## G-013 — LoRA saves memory, not time, and at this scale time differences are noise

**Severity:** low, but it invites a specific misreading · **Affects:** P3's timing column

A frozen base still requires a full forward pass and a full backward pass *through* it to reach the
adapters. LoRA removes optimizer state and gradients for the frozen parameters; it does not remove the
work of propagating through them. Per-step wall-clock is therefore expected to be similar across arms
**by construction**.

On a 2.9 M-parameter model on a shared CPU (G-008), the arm-to-arm timing differences P3 measures are
dominated by machine contention and should not be read as a property of the method. The page reports
the numbers with this stated, rather than omitting them or dressing them up as a speedup.
